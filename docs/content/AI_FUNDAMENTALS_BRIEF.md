# AI Fundamentals for Everyone: brief for module writers (2026-11-18)

**Course:** `ai-fundamentals-everyone`, "AI Fundamentals for Everyone" (beginner, 43 lessons in 8 modules, about 10 hours, 2 projects). Syllabus (titles copied exactly, one lesson per
topic, in order): `src/features/courses/content/catalog/syllabus-ai-fundamentals.ts`. Assembled in `catalog/courses/ai-fundamentals-everyone.ts`; you write ONE module file.

**Who it is for:** everyone with no technical background: students and fresh graduates, teachers, business owners, office workers, freelancers and virtual assistants, career
changers, and anyone curious. **No programming, no mathematics, no jargon without a definition.** Many learners are in the Philippines and work or study in English as a second
language: short sentences, plain words, idioms explained. Use Philippine and international examples (a jeepney route app, a sari-sari store owner, a teacher marking papers, a call-centre
worker, a nurse, a farmer, a student with a thesis, a freelancer with an overseas client, a small online seller, a parent, a shop owner in Manchester, a school in Nairobi, a clinic in
Toronto) without stereotypes, without real or famous people, and without real companies' private details. When a real app is named (Google Maps, Grab, GCash, Facebook, Netflix, Shopee,
Gmail, Google Translate, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot), say what **kind** of thing it does and that such apps **commonly use** AI for it; never state how a company's own system
works unless you are certain and it is public.

## Shared words (use these definitions, in your own sentence shapes, so the whole course agrees)
- **Artificial intelligence (AI):** computer systems that do tasks that usually need human intelligence: recognising speech or pictures, spotting patterns, making predictions, producing language.
- **Machine learning (ML):** a way of building AI where the computer **learns patterns from examples** (data) instead of being given step-by-step rules by a programmer.
- **Deep learning:** machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers; behind most modern speech, image and language AI.
- **Neural network:** a program made of many small connected calculations arranged in layers, **loosely inspired by** the brain (never "works like a brain"). During training the strengths of the connections are adjusted.
- **Model:** what training produces: the learned patterns, stored as numbers, that can be used to make predictions or create content.
- **Training data / training:** the examples a model learns from / the process of learning. Say **using the model** for beginners; the word *inference* only in a depth block.
- **Generative AI:** AI that **creates new content** (text, images, audio, video, code) when you give it a prompt.
- **Large language model (LLM):** a generative AI model trained on very large amounts of text to predict likely next pieces of text, which lets it write, summarise, translate and answer.
- **Chatbot / AI assistant:** an app you talk to, usually powered by an LLM. A chatbot may be narrow (a bank's help bot); an assistant is general (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot).
- **Prompt:** the instruction, question or material you give an AI. **Hallucination:** a fluent, confident answer that is false or made up. **Bias:** a pattern of unfair or skewed results, often inherited from the data or the design.
- **Deepfake:** realistic fake audio, image or video made with AI. **Responsible AI:** using and building AI fairly, safely, transparently and accountably, with a person answerable for the result.
- Never say AI "thinks", "understands", "knows", "wants" or "feels" as a plain fact. Say "predicts", "finds patterns", "produces", "appears to". Do not claim AI is conscious or not conscious: say that
  it has no experiences we can point to and that this course is about what it does. Do not claim more accuracy than a source supports. Do not make predictions about the future as facts ("AI will replace X"): say what
  is changing, what is uncertain and what a person can do.
- **No invented statistics, studies, percentages, dates, quotes or laws.** Use general, well-established facts only (for example: the term "artificial intelligence" was proposed for a 1956 summer workshop at Dartmouth; ChatGPT
  became public in late 2022; the Philippines has a Data Privacy Act of 2012 and a National Privacy Commission). **You have web tools** (load them with ToolSearch `select:WebSearch,WebFetch`): check every dated, legal or product fact you
  write and keep only what you confirmed; where a fact may change, write "at the time of writing (late 2026)" and tell the learner to check the tool's or the country's own current page. **No price figures, plan limits, feature lists
  or button tours** for any tool; no `version` blocks (this course states no product facts that need a dated source): "a free version exists; check what it includes on the tool's own page" is the most you say.
- English only. **Write no Filipino or Tagalog** (the owner's standing rule). Never write dialogue or mascot lines for Tali or Tala.
- Nothing medical, legal or financial **advice**: where a topic touches those, explain the idea and say a qualified person decides. Respectful about every group; examples of bias must be handled carefully and without caricature.

## Every teaching lesson (modules 1 to 7), all checked
- `intro`: `objectives` (2 to 4, verb-first: "Explain…", "Spot…", "Write…") and `why` (one or two sentences: why this matters in the learner's own life). `summary`: one sentence. `minutes`: 12 to 25 (about 15 to 18 is typical). `skills`: your module's id.
- `body`: opens with a **paragraph** (a warm, concrete hook: the learner's own day, a question they have asked), then these exact `heading` blocks, in this order:
  1. **In everyday life**: a short, believable moment (specific people, place, what happened) that shows the idea at work or going wrong. Learners meet the idea before its name.
  2. **How it works**: the explanation. Why before how. Plain words, one honest analogy from everyday life, one new idea at a time, every technical word defined where it first appears. Use short paragraphs and short lists; a `diagram` (Mermaid flowchart or sequence,
     see "Mermaid subset" in WRITING_BRIEF.md: no subgraph or style lines, node labels up to 60 characters, 24 nodes at most) or a `table` where a process or comparison needs one; never decoration. 450 to 750 words of explanation across the shared blocks (the check needs 250; aim for real teaching).
  3. **Real-world examples**: two or three varied examples (Philippine and international, home, school, work, business), each saying what the AI does, what a person still has to do, and what could go wrong. A `table` (situation / what AI does / what a person checks) works well.
  4. **Putting it to use**: practical applications: what the learner can do **tomorrow** with the idea (steps, a checklist, a small habit, how to choose a tool or decide whether to trust a result). Concrete, kind, small.
  5. **Try it with AI**: a `prompt` block the learner can paste into any AI assistant (their own situation in square brackets), written so that **the learner thinks first** (they draft, the AI critiques; or the AI asks questions before answering), plus two or three sentences on what to look for and what
     AI often gets wrong here (it flatters, it is generic, it invents details). The lesson teaches the learner to **check** the answer. The learner may use ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot or Tali, the app's coach, whichever they have; never require a paid plan, and never say which plan or price.
  6. exactly one `takeaways` block (2 to 6 items), **last** in the teaching.
  Optional anywhere: `tip`, `warning` (safety, ethics, "never paste…"), `details` (a "show more" for a deeper note), `list`, `table`, a `demo` block for a hand-written illustration of a prompt and an answer (the app labels it an Illustration; it is **never** a real model's output, so never say it is; its `notice` says what to check).
  **Level pieces** (also `body` blocks, placed right after the idea they explain; they never replace shared teaching, and the first block is for everyone): see "Levels" below.
- `activity` blocks (placed after the takeaways in the file; the app draws them after the quiz), **at least two different kinds per lesson**, from `scenario`, `match`, `tap-reveal`, `arrange`, `fill-blank` only (no code, live-model or practice kinds: they need data this course does not have).
  `scenario`: a decision with consequences, **exactly one `best` option**, one or two `acceptable`, `poor` options that are tempting and explained; `match`: a situation to the idea (never repeating a list printed above), 3 or more unique pairs; `tap-reveal`: reflection cards ("What would you check first?");
  `arrange`: put the steps of a method in order (`language: 'text'`, no tests); `fill-blank`: `language: 'text'`, words of a plan or sentence with a `bank` that contains every answer. Ids `<lesson-id>-<kind>-<n>`, 3 progressive `hints` (a nudge, a pointer, nearly the answer), an `explanation` that says why (never shames), a `concept`.
  Wrong-answer notes explain the mistake without giving the answer away. At least one activity in every lesson must make the learner **apply** the idea to a new situation rather than recall a definition.
- `quiz`: **3 or 4 shared questions plus one newcomer question and one experienced question** (ids `<lesson-id>-q1`…; tagged ones last). 3 or 4 choices (use four wherever you can), one `answer`, an `explanation` of why it is right and why the tempting wrong choice is not. Scenario-based and applied, never trivia, never "all of the above", never "none".
  Every question must be answerable from what the lesson taught. **Vary the correct answer's position** (all four slots, none above half of the course) and **do not make the right answer the longest choice**: wrong choices are as specific as the right one.
- `task`: `{ title, steps (2+), minutes (5 to 20) }`: a small real-world thing to do with the learner's own life, then a one-line reflection. `taskExtra`: extra steps per level (see below).
- Never present AI output as the learner's own proof of skill. Be honest about limits: say when something is a rule of thumb.

## Levels (this course adapts the five levels in `ADAPTATION_BRIEF.md`, which describe **experience with programming and technology**, not intelligence)
- **Newcomer block** (`audience: { max: 'beginner' }`, 80 to 150 words, a `paragraph`, `list` or `tip`): "If all this is new to you": the same idea even more slowly, with a different analogy and every word defined. Warm and never babyish.
- **Experienced block** (`audience: { min: 'advanced' }`, 80 to 150 words): "If you already work with technology": the same idea as someone who uses software at work sees it: precise terms, a trade-off, how it looks in a team, a business or a larger system, and an honest limit. No code. Never show off.
- **Quiz**: one question tagged `audience: { max: 'beginner' }` (checks the plain idea) and one tagged `audience: { min: 'advanced' }` (a realistic work-setting judgement). **taskExtra**: `[{ audience: { max: 'beginner' }, steps: [...] }, { audience: { min: 'advanced' }, steps: [...] }]`.
- Projects: steps with an `audience` (a smaller, scaffolded version for newcomers; a harder, more evidence-based version for experienced learners) and `rubricExtra` for both.
- A shared lesson must still teach every concept on its own. Do not hide anything behind a level.

## Lesson-by-lesson scope (the points every lesson must cover; add more where it helps)
**Module 1.** (1) *What Is Artificial Intelligence?* A plain definition (shared words above), the word's origin (hedge), narrow AI today versus the science-fiction idea of "general" AI (say what exists and what does not), AI versus ordinary software (rules versus learning from examples, in one example), what AI can do well (patterns, speed, language) and badly (common sense, new situations, knowing when it is wrong).
(2) *Where Do We Encounter AI Every Day?* A tour of a normal day: phone unlock, maps and traffic, spam filters, recommendations, translation, voice assistants, banking fraud alerts, photo apps, shopping, navigation and ride apps, customer-service bots; an "AI spotting" habit; which are AI and which are ordinary software (and why that is sometimes hard to tell); what data the app gets in exchange.
(3) *How AI Is Changing the World* (honest, balanced): health, education, farming, transport, customer service, creative work, government services, science; what is changing now versus claimed; benefits and costs; who is affected; no hype and no doom; where the learner fits.
(4) *Common Myths and Misconceptions About AI*: at least eight myths, each with the truth ("AI is always right", "AI thinks like a person", "AI will take every job", "AI is only for programmers", "AI is neutral and has no bias", "AI knows everything on the internet", "if it sounds confident it is correct", "AI learns from my chat in real time" (careful: depends on the tool and settings; say so), "AI is dangerous robots", "using AI is cheating" (depends on the rules of a school or job)). A myth-versus-fact `match` and a sorting scenario.

**Module 2.** (5) *How Computers Learn and Make Decisions*: rules written by a person versus learning from examples; the idea of data, patterns and a prediction; how a computer "decides" (it scores options and picks); the cycle of learn, test, improve; the importance of good examples; a worked example (telling ripe from unripe mangoes, or sorting spam) with no maths.
(6) *What Is Machine Learning?* The definition, the three flavours explained by example (learning from labelled examples; finding groups with no labels; learning by trial and reward), training data and the model, testing on new examples, what "accuracy" means in plain words (and why a model can be 90% right and still unfit for a job; do not quote real figures), overfitting in one everyday analogy (memorising a past exam instead of learning the subject), where ML is used.
(7) *What Is Deep Learning?* How it relates to machine learning (a kind of it), why "deep" means many layers, what it is good at (images, speech, language) and why it needs lots of data and computing power, what it costs (energy, data, explanation is hard), where learners meet it, and what it is not.
(8) *What Are Neural Networks?* Neurons as small calculators, layers, connections with strengths ("weights" in a depth block), learning as adjusting the strengths after mistakes, an everyday analogy (a team passing a message, a mixing board, a kitchen with many cooks tasting and adjusting), a simple picture of a network recognising a handwritten digit or a dog photo; "loosely inspired by the brain, not a brain"; why we cannot always explain a decision. The `ai-neural-network` visual exists (`{ type: 'visual', id: 'ai-neural-network' }`) and may be used if it truly fits; also `ai-model-input-output`.
(9) *Understanding AI Through Everyday Analogies*: a consolidating lesson: AI as a very fast student of examples, a recipe versus taste-testing, a suki-store owner guessing what you will buy, a student who crammed, a GPS that learns from traffic; each analogy shown with where it helps and **where it breaks**; a learner builds their own analogy; connect all of module 2 in one picture (data → learning → model → use → feedback).

**Module 3.** (10) *What Is Generative AI?* Predictive versus generative AI; creates new content, not just labels it; text, images, audio, video, code; what "generate" really means (new combinations from learned patterns, not copying, not understanding); a short history in a line or two (hedged); strengths and limits; where it fits in the AI family tree (AI > ML > deep learning > generative AI): a diagram.
(11) *How AI Generates Text, Images, Audio, and Videos*: at the level of an analogy: text by predicting the next piece of text, images by starting from noise and refining toward what the prompt describes, audio and music by learning patterns of sound, video as many images changing over time plus the difficulty of keeping them consistent; why results vary each time; why each kind makes typical mistakes (extra fingers, garbled text in pictures, odd physics); what a person must still do. No technical detail beyond a depth block.
(12) *Understanding Large Language Models (LLMs)*: trained on very large amounts of text; predicts likely next pieces of text; why that is enough to write, summarise and translate; what a "knowledge cutoff" means; why it does not "look things up" unless it is given a search tool; context (what you give it in the conversation) in plain words; why the same prompt can give different answers; strengths, limits; naming a few families of LLMs without details. (Tokens and context windows: one plain sentence and a pointer to the Generative AI course.)
(13) *What Are AI Chatbots and AI Assistants?* Chatbot versus assistant versus agent in plain words (an agent does steps for you: say it is newer and needs more care), what happens when you send a message (a diagram), memory and privacy in plain words, narrow bots (a bank's help bot) versus general assistants, what they are good at, when to use a person instead, how to tell if you are talking to a bot (honest disclosure).
(14) *Understanding AI Hallucinations and Limitations*: what a hallucination is and why fluent text is not proof; typical kinds (invented facts, fake sources and links, wrong numbers, confident errors in maths and dates, made-up quotes, wrong details about real people); other limits (cutoff, no real-world experience, bias, inconsistency, sensitivity to wording); how to spot and reduce them (ask for sources and check them, ask it to say when unsure, give it the text, ask the same thing a second way, check with a trusted source); when never to rely on it (medical, legal, money decisions without a professional). Include a `demo` block illustration of a made-up citation, clearly an illustration.

**Module 4.** (15) *AI for Personal Productivity*: planning a day or week, reminders and calendars, lists, summarising long messages, meal and budget planning, brain-dumping into a plan; a method (give it your real constraints, ask for a draft, edit it); privacy of personal information; where AI wastes time. Refer to the Time Management and Productivity course by name for the full system. (16) *AI for Writing and Communication*: drafting and improving emails, messages, letters, résumés and posts; tone; translation and plain-language rewriting; ESL support; keeping your own voice; checking facts and names; honesty about AI use where a school or workplace asks; never send what you have not read. (17) *AI for Learning and Research*: explaining ideas at your level, study guides, practice questions, language practice, summarising your own notes, planning research; the risks (made-up sources, shallow understanding, copying); how to use AI as a tutor and not as a shortcut; school rules on AI; verifying with reliable sources; a safe research routine. (18) *AI for Images, Videos, and Creativity*: making and editing images, presentation visuals, short videos, music and voice, brainstorming; what creative work still needs a person; labelling AI-made content; limits and mistakes; likeness and consent; respecting artists. (19) *AI for Business and Entrepreneurship*: small-business uses (product descriptions, customer replies, social posts, simple market research, planning, bookkeeping help, ideas, translation for overseas customers, drafts of policies), what to automate and what not, customer data and privacy, quality control, honesty to customers, starting small with one task and measuring if it helps. (20) *AI for Work and Career Development*: for office workers, teachers, freelancers and job seekers: summarising meetings, drafting reports, learning new skills, résumé and interview practice, a teacher's lesson-planning help, a VA's client communication; company rules and confidentiality; showing your own value; keeping skills sharp; "AI use at work" policy habits. Each of these six lessons: an honest "good for / careful with / not for" table, a method, a real mini-example, and at least one `prompt` the learner can use for their own life. Do not turn lessons into tool tours or price lists.

**Module 5.** (21) *Introduction to ChatGPT and Other AI Assistants*: what an assistant is and how to start (an account, a first message), the main families named without feature lists (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot, Meta AI, and Tali inside Talino as a learning coach), what they share and how they differ in general terms (how they are built into other apps, whether they can search the web or read files: say "some can; check the tool"), free versus paid in one honest paragraph with **no prices**, the conversation window, starting a new chat, privacy settings to look at first, how to choose one for a task. (22) *How to Write Effective Prompts*: the parts of a good prompt (goal, context, audience, format, limits, an example), role-play framing, before and after prompts, a reusable template, iterating (follow-up prompts), common mistakes (too vague, too much at once, hidden assumptions, pasting private data). A `demo` or `table` of weak versus strong prompts. (23) *Asking Better Questions to AI*: clarify your own question first; ask for options and reasoning; ask it to ask you questions; ask for sources and uncertainty; follow-ups ("explain like I am 12", "what would a sceptic say?", "what am I missing?"); neutral wording that does not lead the answer; asking for steps; stating what you already know. (24) *Understanding AI Responses and Verifying Information*: read critically; a verification routine (check facts, numbers, names, dates, quotes and links; compare with two reliable sources; test the advice on a small case; ask a person where it matters); spotting red flags (too smooth, no sources, odd numbers, invented references); lateral reading; what to do when the AI and a source disagree; when to ask an expert; keeping a checking habit. (25) *Hands-on Activity: Complete a Real-World Task Using AI*: a guided walk-through the learner follows **with their own assistant**: choose a real task from a list (a message to a landlord or teacher, a one-week study or meal plan, a short product post, a summary of a long text they paste (nothing private), a simple event plan), write the goal, write the prompt, read the answer, follow up twice, verify, edit, finish, keep a log. This is the lesson where the learner actually uses an assistant for a real everyday task; the `task` repeats it and **P1** (unlocks after this day) is where they submit it. Include a checklist `table` and a scenario about a wrong answer found while checking.

**Module 6.** (26) *What Is Responsible AI?* The idea (fair, safe, private, transparent, accountable, human-centred) in plain words, with an example of each; who is responsible (makers, companies, governments, users); the learner's own responsibility as a user; a simple "stop and ask" routine before using AI for something that affects people; laws and guidance exist and differ by country (mention that the Philippines has a Data Privacy Act and that some regions have specific AI rules; say to check current rules; verify before stating). (27) *AI Bias and Fairness*: where bias comes from (unrepresentative data, past unfairness, how a problem is defined, who builds it); everyday examples handled carefully (a hiring filter that favours past hires, a face-recognition system that works worse for some skin tones, a translation that assumes a doctor is "he", a loan model, an image generator's default people), which are widely reported and described generally (verify; do not invent cases); effects; what a user can do (notice, ask who is missing, test with different wording, not use AI alone for decisions about people, report problems); fairness has more than one meaning; no AI is neutral. (28) *Data Privacy and Security*: what personal data is; what happens to what you type (varies by tool and settings: check the privacy settings and terms); never paste passwords, bank or ID numbers, medical records, private client or student information, or confidential work documents into a public AI tool unless your organisation allows that tool; how to anonymise; phishing and AI-made scams (voice cloning scams: say what to do: call back on a known number, a family code word); the Philippines' Data Privacy Act and the National Privacy Commission named once (verify), and similar laws elsewhere by name only where you are sure; a safe-use checklist. (29) *Copyright, Ownership, and Intellectual Property*: the questions (who owns what you make with AI, was it trained on others' work, can you use it commercially, can it copy someone's work); honest uncertainty: laws differ by country and are still being decided; do not give legal advice; practical habits (read the tool's terms, do not claim others' work as yours, do not ask AI to copy a living artist's style for sale, credit and disclose where required, keep your own drafts and edits as evidence of your contribution, ask a lawyer for anything valuable); schools and plagiarism. (30) *Misinformation, Deepfakes, and AI-Generated Content*: misinformation versus disinformation; how AI makes fake text, images, voices and videos cheaply; how deepfakes are used (scams, fake endorsements, harassment, politics) and how to respond; a checking routine (pause, who made it, other sources, reverse image search idea in plain words, look at context, odd details), why detectors are not reliable; labelling and watermarks are helpful but not proof; what to do if you are targeted (save evidence, report to the platform, tell people you trust, seek help; never share it further); never make a deepfake of someone without consent. (31) *Human Judgment and AI Decision-Making*: what a decision is; decisions AI can support and decisions people must own (hiring, grades, medical and legal outcomes, loans, discipline); automation bias (trusting the machine too much); "human in the loop" and who is accountable; asking for reasons and an appeal; a decision checklist; stakes versus checking effort table; when to say no to AI.
For all of module 6, keep to widely documented, general facts; **describe every example as a type of case** ("hiring tools have been reported to…") unless you have verified a specific public case and can name its source in your own words; add no statistics.

**Module 7.** (32) *How AI Is Transforming Different Industries*: healthcare, education, agriculture, finance and banking, retail and online selling, transport and logistics, manufacturing, customer service and outsourcing, government, media and creative work; for each: what is changing, what people still do, one risk; Philippine and global examples stated generally; hedge. (34) *Skills Everyone Should Develop in the AI Era*: AI literacy, critical thinking and verification, clear communication and prompting, domain knowledge, creativity, collaboration and empathy, adaptability and learning how to learn, ethics and privacy habits, data awareness; for each: what it looks like in practice and one way to build it this month; a self-assessment `tap-reveal` or `table`. (35) *Opportunities and Challenges of AI*: balance sheet: opportunities (access to tutoring and translation, productivity, accessibility tools, science, new small businesses) and challenges (jobs, bias, privacy, misinformation, energy and environment, concentration of power, dependence, the digital divide); who benefits and who is left out; a framework for judging a new AI tool or claim (what problem, whose data, who is accountable, what could go wrong, what is the evidence); avoiding hype and fear. (36) *How to Continue Learning About AI*: a learning plan (habit of trying one new use a week, following a few trustworthy sources, reading tool announcements critically, courses), how to evaluate sources of AI news, communities and school or work policies, how to try new tools safely, where to go next in Talino by course name (Generative AI: From Fundamentals to Real-World Applications; AI Basics for Tech; Time Management and Productivity), and a 30-day plan.

## Quality bar
- Substance: each lesson 600 to 1,100 words of shared teaching plus the level pieces, a real analogy, at least two real-world examples, practical steps, a copyable prompt, 2+ activity kinds and a quiz that tests judgement. No filler, no padding, no placeholders, no "In this lesson we will…" openings, no lists of definitions as the whole lesson.
- Tone: a patient teacher; second person; short paragraphs; kind; honest about limits; engaging, not textbook-like. Define every term where it first appears. **Write so it reads well aloud** (the lesson is narrated by a text-to-speech voice): avoid long tables as the only place an idea lives, avoid symbols and abbreviations, spell out numbers sensibly.
- Examples are believable, respectful and varied; no stereotypes; no real people; no real companies' private details; no medical, legal or financial advice.
- Consistent terminology with the shared words above and with the other modules (the syllabus list tells you what the other modules teach: refer to them, never re-teach).
- Report at the end: lessons written, activities by kind, quiz questions, claims you verified (and how) and those you cut or hedged, anything you were unsure of, the output of the check. Do not claim to have run a live AI engine; you cannot.
