## Every teaching lesson (modules 1 to 10; all checked)
- `intro` (2 to 4 verb-first objectives, a one-to-two-sentence `why`), `summary` (one sentence, the lesson's short description), `minutes` **10 to 14** (the whole course is about 14 hours; do not pad), `skills`.
- `body`: about **450 to 700 words of shared teaching** (shared = blocks with no `audience`; `lesson-structure` needs at least 250, aim for real teaching, not definitions). It opens with a **paragraph**, then
  these exact headings, in this order:
  1. **A real situation**: a concrete, believable moment (named people, a few lines of what happened, what it cost in time or trust). Mix Philippine and international settings across the module.
  2. **How it works**: the idea for a beginner: why before how, one honest analogy if it helps, then a method the learner can use tomorrow (steps, a checklist, a frame). Define every unfamiliar word the
     first time it appears and put key terms in **bold** (they become key terms in the app). Always separate **what a rule decides** (deterministic: the same input always gives the same result) from **what AI
     decides** (a judgment that can vary and can be wrong) wherever the lesson touches both.
  3. **Worked example**: a practical example the learner can follow: the **sample input data** (a `code` block, `language` `text`, `json` or `csv`, fictional data only), the **trigger and steps** (a numbered
     `list`, or a `diagram`), the **sample prompt** where AI is used, and the **expected output**. Use the shared running example (below) where it fits. Where a lesson has a diagram requirement, draw it here.
  4. **Level pieces** (blocks with `audience`; see ADAPTATION_BRIEF): a gentler walk-through for newcomers (`{ max: 'beginner' }`) and a "in a real workplace / at scale" variation for experienced learners (`{ min: 'advanced' }`).
     They go before "AI in Action" (any position inside the teaching is fine for the checks, but keep them near the part they extend).
  5. **AI in Action**: one `demo` block (`{ type: 'demo', title, prompt, output, notice }`; prompt at most 1,200 characters, output 2,000, notice 800), a **hand-written illustration**, never presented as a real
     model's answer (the app always badges it "Illustration"). `notice` says what to check in the output (a wrong figure, an invented fact, a field it assumed). Do not use phrases like "ChatGPT replied" or "real output" in `title` or `notice`.
  6. **Try it yourself**: a `prompt` block the learner can copy into their own AI assistant with their own situation in [brackets], and **the learner thinks first** (they draft, the AI critiques; or the AI asks
     questions) wherever it fits. Then a short step-by-step for doing the same by hand or in an automation platform they can reach (optional, never required), and **a troubleshooting line** ("if the answer is wrong
     in this way, do this"). A `warning` or `tip` block is welcome.
  7. **Next steps**: a `list` of two to four small, concrete things to do in the next day or week.
  8. exactly one `takeaways` block (2 to 6 items), last.
  Optional: `tip`, `warning`, `details`, `list`, `table`, `visual` (existing visual ids only; `ai-agent-loop`, `ai-context-window`, `ai-prompt-anatomy` fit some lessons), `diagram`.
- **No `version` blocks, no product facts.** When you name ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Zapier, Make, n8n, Google Workspace (Gmail, Sheets, Forms, Calendar, Docs, Drive), Microsoft 365 (Outlook, Excel, Teams, Forms,
  SharePoint), Notion, Trello, Airtable, WhatsApp or Messenger, say what kind of tool it is and what it is for, never a price, a plan limit, "it is free", a feature list, an app count or a button tour. Say "check what your own plan
  includes" and "check the tool's own pricing page" instead. It is correct to teach **how such platforms are usually priced** in general words: many offer a limited free tier or a trial, many charge by usage
  (tasks, operations, runs or executions), a tool that can be self-hosted trades a monthly fee for your own setup and care; **all of this changes, so verify**. The core course never needs a paid plan.
- **Diagrams**: `{ type: 'diagram', format: 'mermaid', source: 'flowchart LR\n  A[...] --> B[...]', caption }`. Read the Mermaid subset in WRITING_BRIEF.md and `visuals/diagrams/mermaid.ts`: flowchart (TD or LR), sequenceDiagram,
  classDiagram, erDiagram only; **no `subgraph`, `style`, `classDef`**; ids have no hyphens; labels short; at most about 10 nodes. A diagram must show the real mechanism (trigger → AI step → condition → action →
  human approval → log), not decorate. A flowchart with a `{decision}` node is the way to show conditions. The test checks every diagram parses; `npx jest` tells you the error and line.
- **Tables** are good for comparisons (rules vs AI, tool kinds, before and after); every row the same width.
- `activity` blocks (placed after the takeaways in the file; the app draws them after the quiz), **at least one per lesson and across the module a mix**: `workflow-builder` (below), `scenario` (a decision with
  consequences: exactly one `best`, one or two `acceptable`, `poor` ones that are tempting and explained), `match` (a situation to the idea, never a repeat of a printed list; use `labels`), `arrange` (put the steps of a
  method in order; `language: 'text'`, no tests), `fill-blank` (`language: 'text'`, a `bank` including distractors), `tap-reveal` (reflection cards). **Not used in this course:** `predict-output`, `fix-bug`,
  `code-challenge`, `sql-challenge`, `roleplay`, `message-review`, `prompt-lab`, `planning` (the test rejects them). Each has 3 progressive hints, an explanation, a concept; ids `<lesson-id>-<kind>-<n>`.
- `quiz`: **3 shared questions** (no `audience`) plus **one question for newcomers** (`audience: { max: 'beginner' }`) **and one for experienced learners** (`audience: { min: 'advanced' }`): scenario questions that
  test judgement ("What is the best next step?", "What is wrong with this workflow?"), 3 or 4 choices, varied correct slot, an `explanation` that says why, never trivia, never "all of the above", never a
  question the lesson did not teach. **The right answer must not always be the longest**; make wrong choices as detailed and plausible as the right one. Ids `<lesson-id>-q<n>`.
- `task`: a short real-world thing to do (map a task, write a prompt, sketch a workflow on paper, test a rule on three samples) with 2+ steps, 5 to 20 minutes, using the learner's own life or work, then a short
  reflection. `taskExtra` (an array of `{ audience, steps }`): a scaffold for newcomers and a harder variation for experienced learners.

### The `workflow-builder` activity (the course's hands-on tool; rule-checked, never a model)
Shape (see `activities/types.ts` and the model near line 970 of `generative-ai-applications/module-05.ts`): `{ id, kind: 'workflow-builder', title, prompt, concept, situation, parts, rules, solution, minSteps, maxSteps,
simulation: { input, steps, output }, hints (3), explanation }`. The learner picks steps from the palette and puts them in order; the rules are checked by code, and when the workflow passes, `simulation` plays a
sample request through the steps ("Simulated run, not a real model").
- **Palette 8 to 12 parts** across the lanes `input`, `ai`, `tool`, `human`, `output` (the checker needs at least an `ai` part and a `human` part in the palette, 5+ parts, 2+ rules with at least one `require` and
  one `forbid`). Include **2 to 4 tempting wrong parts**: an AI step that sends the message with no review, a step that pastes a password or a customer's card number into a prompt, "trust the AI's number" with no check, no log,
  an automation that never stops on an error, an AI that decides a refund. Rules: `require` (parts that must be in), `forbid` (unsafe parts), `before` (`[a, b]`: a first, e.g. human approval before sending). Every rule's
  `message` explains **why** in one sentence, not just what.
- `solution` is one valid order and **must satisfy all rules** (`validateActivity` runs it); `simulation.steps` has a hand-written line for **each solution part id**; `input` and `output` are a fictional sample.
  Every workflow ends with a human decision wherever the outcome touches money, a person or reputation. Part ids kebab-case. Vary the situation across your module (not the same shop each time).
- Use them for what they are good at: ordering, spotting the step that must not be automated, spotting the missing approval or log.

### Honesty rules (structural)
- Every business, customer, invoice, lead, ticket and number is **fictional** and, where a learner could mistake it, labelled as an example. Numbers in ROI or time-saved examples are labelled "example numbers".
- **Never present AI output as proof of the learner's skill or as correct.** Always show the check: what to verify, what AI got wrong or could get wrong.
- Never suggest a workflow that sends money, deletes data, replies to a customer about a refund or a complaint, posts publicly, hires or fires, gives medical, legal or tax advice, or uses a customer's private data in an
  unapproved service **without a human approving it first**. This is the course's backbone; say it in every module where it applies and make the strong examples follow it.
- Never promise income, clients, "set it and forget it", or that AI "replaces" a person. Time-saved claims are estimates the learner measures.
- Say plainly that Talino does not connect to Zapier, Make, n8n, Google or Microsoft; the in-app builder is a rule-checked simulation, and building in a real platform is optional and outside the app.
- Privacy: sample prompts contain fictional data only; teach redaction (remove names, numbers and addresses before sending anything to a service you have not approved).
- Nothing medical, legal or financial-advice. Where a situation calls for it (health complaint, a legal threat, a tax question, a data breach), the lesson says a professional is the right person.
- No invented statistics, studies, percentages, market sizes or quotes. Describe techniques in your own words.
- **English only.** Write no Filipino or Tagalog (owner's standing rule). Plain, warm, simple English an ESL learner can follow. Short sentences. Define idioms. Never write dialogue or lines for Tali or Tala.

## The shared running example: Pili & Pine Candles
A **fictional** online candle shop in Naga City, Camarines Sur, selling around the Philippines and to a few customers in the US and Australia. Owner **Ana Villanueva**; helper **Josh Ramos** packs orders. Used in
Modules 6, 8, 9 and 11 (and anywhere it fits); other lessons use other businesses. **Keep these facts identical everywhere:**
- Order numbers look like `PP-1042`. Products: soy candles in jars (three scents), gift sets, refill packs.
- **Inquiry form fields:** `customer_name`, `email`, `order_number` (optional), `message`, `submitted_at`.
- **AI output (JSON) for each inquiry:** `{ "category": "order_status | shipping_delay | product_question | refund_request | complaint | wholesale | other", "urgency": "low | medium | high", "needs_human": true | false, "summary": "one sentence", "suggested_reply": "a draft, never sent by AI" }`.
- **Sheet columns:** Received, Name, Email, Order number, Category, Urgency, Needs human, Summary, Suggested reply, Status (`Pending review`, `Approved`, `Rejected`, `Escalated`), Approved by, Sent at.
- **Rules (deterministic, not AI):** category `complaint`, `refund_request` or `wholesale`, urgency `high`, or `needs_human: true` → status `Escalated` and Ana is notified; **the AI never decides a refund, never promises a date it
  was not given, never sends anything by itself.** Everything else waits as `Pending review` until a person approves.
- **Three sample inquiries:** (1) "Hi, where is my order PP-1042? It has been 9 days." (shipping_delay, medium, a draft that asks Josh to check the courier status, promises no date); (2) "Do your soy candles have a scent that is not too
  strong? I get bad headaches." (product_question, `needs_human: true`: no health advice from AI, Ana answers); (3) "I want my money back. The jar arrived cracked and this is the second time." (refund_request / complaint, high, escalated).
- Other fictional businesses you may use freely: Kapeng Bicol (coffee shop), Dr. Reyes Family Dental Clinic, BrightPath Tutoring Centre, Harbour & Reed (a small UK consultancy), Maple Street Goods (a US online shop), Lakeview Diner,
  Mia Santos Design (a freelance designer), a school registrar's office, a BPO team lead.
- Other sample data (use in Modules 3, 4, 9): an invoice (`INV-2026-0418`, from "Bicol Glass & Jars Supply" to Pili & Pine Candles, dated 2026-11-03, due 2026-12-03, line items, total PHP 18,450.00; a second version whose total does not
  equal the sum of its lines and whose bank account differs from the supplier's last invoice, for the "flag suspicious" lesson); a week of sales rows; a meeting transcript; an email thread.

## Module notes: what each lesson must teach (the titles are the contract; these say what makes them practical)
**Module 1 (days 1-7).** Plain definitions and everyday examples. L1 Automation (a rule that runs without you; the paper-form-to-spreadsheet example; the idea of a trigger). L2 AI automation (rules plus a step that reads, writes,
sorts or decides on messy input; AI is a *step* in a workflow, not the workflow). L3 AI vs traditional automation: **the heart of the course**: a table (input type, same result every time?, who is accountable, failure mode, cost of a
wrong answer, when to choose which), the diagram "Traditional automation vs AI automation", and the principle "use rules wherever a rule is enough; use AI only where the input is messy; keep a person where being wrong is costly".
L4 How AI changes work (what moves from doing to checking; the new skills: describing, checking, deciding; honest about jobs and about what it does badly). L5 Common tasks (email, meetings, documents, spreadsheets, social posts, leads,
support, bookkeeping admin, scheduling, reports; a sorting activity). L6 No-code and low-code (what they are, what a "connector" and "template" are, when low-code is the right step up; no tool tour). L7 Identifying opportunities (the
practical: a task inventory, the score of frequency × minutes × rule-ness × risk, the "do not automate yet" list; this lesson's task is the module's practical activity: *list five repetitive tasks from your day or workplace and
classify each as traditional automation, AI-powered automation, or leave it manual*; give a worked table of five tasks for a fictional person). Diagram requirement: at least three diagrams in the module (traditional vs AI, a trigger-action
basics flow, the opportunity scoring flow or a "rule or AI?" decision flowchart).

## Projects (modules 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11): the `Project` shape
`Project` in `types.ts`: `id`, `title`, `summary`, `outcome` (the proof it gives a client or employer), `unlockAfterDay`, `steps` (6 to 10, ids `<project-id>-s<n>`, each a clear instruction; **at least one step with an `audience`**: a scaffolded one for newcomers (`{ max: 'beginner' }`), and a harder one for experienced learners (`{ min: 'advanced' }`)), `submission` (`kind: 'text'`; `label`, `placeholder`, and a `hint` saying what a good submission contains and that the coach reviews it against the rubric), `rubric` (3 to 6 criteria that read like what a reviewer checks), `rubricExtra` (`[{ audience, items }]`).
