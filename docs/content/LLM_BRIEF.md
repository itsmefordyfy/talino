# How Large Language Models Work: brief for module writers

**Course:** `how-llms-work` (intermediate tier, "beginner to intermediate" learners; 77 lessons in 11 modules; one project). Design (read first):
`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-11-18-how-llms-work-course-design.md`. Syllabus: `src/features/courses/content/catalog/syllabus-llm.ts` (**lesson titles copied exactly, one lesson per topic, in order**).
Subtitle: *Discover what happens behind the scenes when you talk to ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini.* Learners: students and graduates, software developers, AI enthusiasts, technical and
non-technical professionals, freelancers and VAs, entrepreneurs. **Basic AI knowledge helps but is not required. No programming. No advanced mathematics** (small numbers, pictures
and analogies; no formulas beyond "multiply, add, compare"). Many learners are in the Philippines: plain English an ESL reader can follow, short sentences, idioms defined. Examples span
jobs (a bakery owner, a teacher, a VA, a clinic receptionist, a freelance designer, a small shop, a developer), never only software teams.

**Where this course sits:** `AI Fundamentals for Everyone` (what AI is, how to use assistants; no inner workings) → **this course (how it works inside)** → developer courses. So: do not re-teach
"what is AI", "what is machine learning" or "how to write a prompt" (recall them in one sentence at most: "you met this in AI Fundamentals for Everyone"). Do not teach prompt writing or building
(`Generative AI: From Fundamentals to Real-World Applications` and the developer courses own that); here the question is always **why does it work that way inside?**, then "so what does that mean for
me when I use one?".

## Accuracy and honesty (a person checks these; the course teaches mechanism, so mistakes here are the worst defect)
- **Teach the established principles correctly and modestly.** A language model is a very large neural network trained to predict the next token in text; generating a reply repeats that prediction, one token at a time, feeding each chosen token back in. The Transformer (self-attention) is the architecture most LLMs build on (introduced in the 2017 paper
  *Attention Is All You Need*). Training adjusts **parameters** (weights) to reduce prediction error. Instruction tuning, RLHF (human preference ratings used to improve the model; first widely described for assistants with InstructGPT, 2022) and DPO (a simpler way to learn from preference pairs, published 2023) are described as the idea and the purpose, not as any company's recipe.
- **No unsupported claims about proprietary systems.** Never state a product's parameter count, training data, training cost, architecture details, context size, release date, benchmark score, price or ranking. Say "is not public" or "differs by model" and put such a point in the lesson's "What is settled, and what varies" block. Names of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Mistral etc. are fine as examples of LLM families; do not compare them, rank them or describe their
  features from memory.
- **Do not fabricate technical specifications.** Numbers in a lesson are labelled *invented* ("made-up numbers for teaching") and are small. Typical-scale statements are allowed only when hedged and well established ("modern LLMs have billions of parameters"; "trained on a very large amount of text"); no precise figures.
- **Analogies are labelled as analogies** and their limit is named once ("this is only an analogy: the model does not 'know' like a person").
- Say plainly where something is **an open research question or not fully understood** (why large models do what they do inside; how much a model "understands"). Do not anthropomorphise as fact: "the model predicts…", "appears to…", never "the model knows / wants / believes / lies" without qualifying.
- No invented statistics, studies, quotes or company claims. Invented examples are labelled invented where they could be taken for fact. No real or famous person in an example. Case studies are invented composites.
- Medical, legal and financial examples show *why a human must verify*, never give advice. Educational, not marketing.
- Tali and Tala: never write their dialogue. Use "an AI assistant" for the learner's own tool. **English only: write no Filipino or Tagalog** (owner's standing rule).
- Never present an AI output as proof of the learner's skill.

## Module notes (what each module must do beyond its lesson titles)
1. **Introduction (5):** activity in 1.1 or 1.2 where the learner sorts everyday applications into "powered by an LLM" / "not" (a `match` or `scenario`), and a "traditional software vs LLM response" comparison (a `table` and a `scenario`) in 1.2. 1.3 compares ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and "families" by *what they have in common*, not by ranking. 1.5 myths: each myth is a tap-reveal card.
2. **Learn from data (6):** the module's walk-through: a simplified example of learning language patterns from a tiny corpus (count which word follows which in five invented sentences, 2.4); parameters and weights with a tiny analogy (dials/knobs), the training loop (guess, compare, adjust, repeat) as a `diagram`; 2.6 "more data is not automatically better" (quality, duplicates, bias, noise) with honest limits. Training data sources are described by *kind* (books, web pages, code, licensed and curated data): do not state what any model was trained on.
3. **Tokens, tokenization, embeddings (6):** break sample sentences into tokens by hand with an invented small vocabulary (3.2), how rare words split (3.3), why tokens ≠ words, languages and numbers use tokens differently (described qualitatively, no counts for real tokenizers), embeddings as lists of numbers where similar meaning is nearby (3.4 to 3.6) using a tiny 2- or 3-number invented table.
4. **Transformer (7):** progressively: the picture (4.1), why it mattered (parallel processing of the whole sequence vs reading one word at a time; scaling; keep modest), self-attention with a sentence like "The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big" (4.3 to 4.4), query/key/value with the **library or search analogy** and invented small numbers (4.5), positional encoding (4.6), layers (4.7: stacked layers, each refining the representation; attention + feed-forward; residual ideas only by analogy). The learning activity: use the `llm-attention` visual to see attention connect words.
5. **Generating responses (7):** 5.1 to 5.3 are three *different* lessons, not three copies: (1) the prediction (probabilities over the vocabulary), (2) why next-token prediction is enough to produce fluent text and its consequences, (3) the loop step by step with a worked example. 5.4 temperature, 5.5 top-k and top-p, 5.6 same prompt different answers, 5.7 inference (what is happening when you press send; the cost of generating long answers; the key idea that the model is not retrained by your chat). Activities simulate next-token prediction with invented tables and ask the learner to predict what changes when a setting changes.
6. **Training, fine-tuning, alignment (8):** a pipeline `diagram` (pretraining → fine-tuning/instruction tuning → preference learning → evaluation) in 6.1; the **base model vs instruction-tuned assistant** comparison (6.4 is the module's learning activity: show an invented base-model continuation and an invented assistant answer to the same text, as `demo` blocks labelled Illustration, and a `scenario`). RLHF and DPO explained as "people say which of two answers is better; the model is nudged toward the preferred kind" with the honest limits (people disagree, reward can be gamed, sycophancy). Evaluation (6.8): benchmarks and human review described as *ideas*, with their limits (no scores).
7. **Context, memory, limitations (8):** the module's learning activity is changing context (use `ai-context-window` in 7.1 or 7.4 and an activity "which fact is still in view?"). Hallucination (7.5), knowledge cutoff (7.6), truth (7.7): LLMs model likely text, not facts, unless connected to sources; list strategies (7.8): give context, ask for sources then check them, ask it to say when unsure, break tasks up, verify with a trusted source, use retrieval or tools.
8. **RAG (8):** the module's learning activity is walking a RAG workflow on a made-up three-paragraph document (retrieve, show the chosen passages, answer from them) (8.2 or 8.6, an `arrange` plus a `scenario`, `ai-rag` visual). 8.3 vector databases explained without products; 8.7 RAG vs fine-tuning (a `table`: what each changes, when each fits, limits); 8.8 real-world uses (invented composites).
9. **Multimodal (6):** the module's learning activity: practical examples of image, audio and text understanding (descriptions of what the model receives and returns for an invented photo, voice note and clip, as `scenario` and `match`). Explain *how* images become patches/numbers, speech becomes text or sound tokens, video as frames over time, at the level of the idea; **do not claim how a specific product does it**; mention limits (misreading images, accents, noise, hallucinated detail) and privacy. `workflow-builder` allowed.
10. **Real world and the future (8):** each domain lesson (10.1 to 10.4) shows where an LLM helps, where a human **must** verify (the module's learning activity: identify situations that need human verification, and analyse how an LLM could solve one real problem), and what to be careful of (no medical, legal or financial advice given). 10.5 agents and tool calling and 10.6 function calling: the idea (the model *asks* to use a tool by naming it with arguments; the application runs it and returns the result; the model never runs code itself) with `ai-agent-loop` and a `diagram`, and an invented function schema in a `table` or list (not code). 10.7 limits, security (prompt injection explained simply, data leakage, over-trust) and responsible AI. 10.8 the future: written as questions to keep asking, no predictions as fact. `workflow-builder` encouraged in 10.5 to 10.7.
11. **(written last, from the finished modules)**: see the instructions given to that writer.
