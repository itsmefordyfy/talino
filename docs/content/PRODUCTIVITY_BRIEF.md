# Time Management and Productivity: brief for module writers (2026-11-17)

**Course:** `time-management-productivity`, "Time Management and Productivity" (beginner, 43 lessons in 10 modules, about 10 hours, 3 projects). Design:
`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-11-17-productivity-course-design.md` (read it: the tools, what is graded by code, what is honest about AI). Syllabus:
`src/features/courses/content/catalog/syllabus-productivity.ts` (**lesson titles copied exactly, one lesson per topic, in order**).

**Subtitle:** Work smarter, stay focused, and achieve more. Learners: developers, IT people, freelancers and VAs, builders, teachers, career changers, fresh graduates, students.
Their examples must span those lives (a freelancer with three clients, a teacher marking papers, a student with exams and a part-time job, a VA across time zones, a junior
developer with a sprint, a parent working from home, a shop owner), not only office workers. Many learners are in the Philippines and work with clients abroad, in English. Use names and
places naturally and without stereotypes; no real or famous people, no real companies' private details.

You write ONE module (your number is in your instructions). **Read first, in this order:** `docs/content/WRITING_BRIEF.md` (the lesson contract, activity rules, accuracy rules; its
"DIRECTION" part about code does not apply, this course has no code), `docs/content/ADAPTATION_BRIEF.md` (the five learner levels and the `audience` pieces every lesson carries),

depth and shape**: `src/features/courses/content/catalog/courses/soft-skills-workplace/module-04.ts` (its lesson parts are almost the same; this course adds "AI in Action" and "Next steps").

## Every lesson (all checked)
- `intro` (2 to 4 verb-first objectives, one-to-two-sentence `why`), `summary` (one sentence), `minutes` 10 to 25 (the course totals about 10 hours; most lessons 12 to 16), `skills` (your skill id).
- `body` (about 450 to 750 words of explanation across the shared blocks: explanation, not a list of definitions; `lesson-structure` needs at least 250 shared words, but aim for the real teaching): opens with a **paragraph**, then these exact headings, in this order:
  1. **A real situation**: a concrete, believable moment that shows the skill failing or working (specific names, a few lines of what happened, what it cost).
  2. **How it works**: the idea explained for a beginner, why before how, one honest analogy if it helps, then a method the learner can use tomorrow (steps, a checklist, a simple frame). Name a technique (Eisenhower matrix, ABC, time blocking, Pomodoro, the two-minute rule, implementation intentions, habit stacking, the planning fallacy) only when you are sure it is real and **describe it in your own words**, and say plainly where it is a rule of thumb rather than proven science. No invented statistics, studies, percentages or quotes. A `visual` block for the course's tools goes here or under "Weak and strong examples" (see the module requirements).
  3. **Weak and strong examples**: the same situation handled badly and well, as a `table` (weak / strong / why it works) or a `diagram`; the strong version is one among several good ones, not "the" answer.
  4. Level pieces (`audience` on blocks, see the adaptation brief): a gentler walk-through for beginners (`{ max: 'beginner' }`) and a "in your real job" or harder variation for advanced learners (`{ min: 'advanced' }`).
  5. **AI in Action**: a short demonstration of how AI would help with this lesson's skill, **always labelled "Illustration, written by hand, not a live AI's output"** (a paragraph that says so, then the example in a `details` block or a `table` or a `list`). Show the prompt a learner would send and an abridged answer, then what to check in it (a wrong number, an invented task, something it assumed). Never present it as real output.
  6. **Try it with AI**: a `prompt` block the learner copies into an AI assistant (their own situation in the brackets), written so that **the learner does the thinking first** (they draft, the AI critiques; or the AI asks questions and does not decide for them), plus one or two sentences on what to look for and what AI gets wrong here (it flatters, it is generic, it invents tasks or deadlines, it hides disagreement). The lesson teaches the learner to **check** what the AI says. Where a prompt in the playground fits (`ai-prompt-playground`: daily plan, weekly plan, sort tasks, break down a task, meeting notes, project schedule, review my week) point to it by name instead of repeating it.
  7. **Next steps**: a `list` of two to four concrete things to do in the next day or week (small, specific, kind).
  8. exactly one `takeaways` block (2 to 6 items), last.
  Optional: `tip`, `warning`, `details`, `list`. **No `code` blocks, no `version` notes** (nothing here states a product fact: when you name Google Calendar, Notion, Trello, Todoist, Microsoft To Do, Jira, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Zapier, Make or n8n, say what kind of tool it is and what it is for, never a price, a plan limit, a feature list or a button tour, and tell the learner to check the tool's own page. **The core course never needs a paid plan**; say a free option exists only as "check what the free version includes").
- `activity` blocks (placed after the takeaways in the file; the app draws them after the quiz), **at least two kinds per lesson**: `scenario` (a decision with consequences: exactly one `best` option, one or two `acceptable`, `poor` ones that are tempting and explained), `match` (a situation to the idea, never a repeat of a printed list), `tap-reveal` (reflection cards), `arrange` (put the steps of a method in order; `language: 'text'`, no tests), `fill-blank` (`language: 'text'`, words of a plan with a `bank`), **`planning`** (the graded tools, below), `roleplay` / `message-review` (the AI practice, below). `predict-output`, `fix-bug`, `code-challenge`, `sql-challenge` are not for this course. Each has 3 progressive hints, an explanation, a concept; ids `<lesson-id>-<kind>-<n>` (`planning` ids use the kind `planning`). Tone: the explanation says why, it never shames.
- `quiz`: 3 to 5 scenario questions that test judgement ("What is the best next step?", "What is wrong with this plan?"), 3 or 4 choices, a varied correct slot, an `explanation`; never trivia, never "all of the above", never a question the lesson did not teach; the right answer must not always be the longest.
- `task`: a short real-world thing to do (plan, observe, try, review) with 2+ steps, 5 to 20 minutes, using the learner's own life, then a reflection. `taskExtra` for the levels.
- Honesty: **never present AI output as the learner's proof of skill**; scores in the app come from rules (planning activities) or from the learner's own words (practice). Say when a technique is a rule of thumb. Stay practical and kind: no hustle culture, no "wake at 5 AM" advice, no guilt, no claims about "what successful people do". **Never encourage excessive work, constant tracking or tools as a substitute for rest**; module 10 and every planning scenario keep breaks and personal life in.

## Interactive tools and where they go
Visual ids (embed with `{ type: 'visual', id }`): `eisenhower-matrix` (the learner's own tasks, kept on the phone), `daily-schedule-builder` (their own day, kept), `pomodoro-timer`, `ai-prompt-playground`. Each is a real tool: send the learner to use it with their own tasks, and say what to try ("add three tasks you are avoiding and sort them"). Use the ids your module needs (see the table in `MODULE_REQUIREMENTS`) and any other that truly fits.

### The `planning` activity (graded by rules, never by a model)
Shape: `{ id, kind: 'planning', title, prompt, hints (3), explanation, concept, challenge }` with `challenge` either
`{ mode: 'prioritize', tasks: MatrixTaskSpec[], passRatio? }` (the Eisenhower sort; 5 to 12 tasks; at least three of the four boxes used; each task has `expected`, optional `alsoAcceptable`, and a `why` that explains the call in a sentence **and does not say the box name** because it is shown after a wrong try) or
`{ mode: 'schedule', scenario: ScheduleScenario, solution }` (a day: `windowStart`/`windowEnd` in minutes since midnight (write `const H = (h: number, m = 0) => h * 60 + m` at the top of your file), `fixed` commitments, `tasks` with an estimate, a priority, optional `deadline`, `mustDo`, `okActions`, optional `rules` and `interruption`).
- A schedule scenario must be **realistic and solvable**: a window of 8 to 10 hours, 2 to 4 fixed commitments including a meal, 4 to 7 tasks, one must-do with a deadline, and at least one task that cannot fit (so the learner must decide: defer, delegate, decline or automate; give `okActions` that make the sensible choices). Include an `interruption` in the scenarios that teach interruptions (the learner rebuilds the plan after it arrives).
- The `solution` is a reference plan that **must pass the scenario's own rules** (`validateActivity` runs it: no overlaps, inside the day, must-do done in time and long enough, a break before any stretch of work (tasks and meetings) passes `maxFocusRun` (default 120 minutes), rest at least `minBreakTotal` (default 30, meals count), free time at least `minSlack` (default 20), task minutes no more than `maxPlannedMinutes` (default 360), every task left out has a decision, the interruption is honoured). Block ids `s1`, `s2`...; task blocks carry `taskId`; split a long task over two blocks around a break; allocate at least 90% and at most 150% of the estimate.
- Vary the scenarios across the course in role, setting and what gives (a freelancer's Friday, a teacher's marking night, a student's exam week, a developer's sprint day, a VA with a client in another time zone, a parent's work-from-home day). Do not reuse the two samples. Do not hide the rules: the screen shows them. Hints coach the approach (where to put the must-do, why a break), never the finished plan.
- The learner's plan is kept while the app is open; a failed check never wipes it.

### The AI practice (role-play and message coach)
`{ kind: 'roleplay', scenarioId, id, title, prompt, hints (2+), explanation, concept }` and `{ kind: 'message-review', taskId, ... }`. **Every item you write in `practice/scenarios/productivity/module-NN.ts` is used by exactly one activity, and every such activity names an item that exists** (ids start `prod-`, kebab-case, unique across the course: `prod-<kebab>`). The server reads items by id and never sends their private fields to the phone, so write for **an AI that must play a person**. Read `practice/types.ts` closely and one module of `practice/scenarios/module-04.ts` (Soft Skills time management) as the craft model, and **do not repeat its scenarios** (a manager who adds work to a full week; a message that declines or renegotiates a deadline are taken: pick different people and moments).
- **RoleplayScenario**: `setting` (2 to 4 sentences), one-sentence `goal` (an outcome, not a script), a partner (first name, title, personality, **`private`**: what they want, fear or know; **`reactions.good` / `reactions.poor`**), the partner's `opening` line, `minTurns` 3 to 5, `maxTurns` 6 to 12, 3 to 6 `criteria`, `strongSigns` (3 to 6), `traps` (3 to 5), `difficulty`. The partner is a **real person with a believable position**, not a villain and not a pushover. Never put the ideal reply in any field. For an "accountability partner" the partner is a friend or study buddy who cares and is honest, not a coach reading a script and not an app; they never diagnose or claim to know the learner's habits.
- **MessageTask**: `situation`, `brief`, `channel`, `received` (when replying), `mustInclude` (2 to 6 short items), `targetWords { min, max }`, 3 to 7 `criteria` from the standard measures plus others that fit. Mix difficulty (one `gentle` and one `demanding` if you write three or more items).
- Required AI practice per module is in `MODULE_REQUIREMENTS` (counts of role-play and message-review activities). Suggested items: M3 a message that declines or delegates a request politely and offers an alternative (a favour from a senior colleague); M4 a role-play where a teammate keeps pulling you into quick questions during a planned focus block; M5 a role-play with an honest friend you ask to hold you accountable after a missed day; M6 a role-play with a colleague who interrupts your deep-work block; M8 three message tasks (a weekly progress report from a list of what was done; meeting notes turned into action items with owners and dates; an email request turned into a task list and a reply that confirms scope), **the learner writes it in their own words and the coach reviews**; M9 a role-play with a client in another time zone who wants a call at your midnight, a role-play with two stakeholders whose deadlines conflict, and a message that moves a deadline; M10 a role-play with a manager about workload and boundaries (workplace-appropriate, no medical advice) and a message that is a weekly review in the learner's own words. You may add more.
- Honest about speaking: none of this course's items is `speaking`.

## Level pieces

## Projects (modules 7, 8, 9)
`Project` shape in `types.ts`: `id` `time-management-productivity-p<n>`, `title`, `summary`, `outcome`, `unlockAfterDay`, `steps` (6 to 10, ids `<project-id>-s<n>`, each a clear instruction; include steps with an `audience` for beginners and advanced learners), `submission` (`kind: 'text'`: the learner pastes their own written work and a short reflection in their own words, with a `label`, `placeholder` and a `hint` saying what a good submission contains), `rubric` (3 to 6 criteria that read like what a reviewer checks) and `rubricExtra`. Each project has clear instructions, expected deliverables, evaluation criteria and the feedback it gets (the coach reviews it against the rubric; say so in the `hint`). **Never ask the learner to submit what an AI wrote as their own**; the rubric rewards the learner's own choices, trade-offs and reflection ("what you changed after checking the AI's suggestion and why"), and it rewards realistic plans (breaks, slack, personal time) and never a plan that is full to the minute.
- **P1 My Personal Productivity System** (module 7, after day 30): their goals (personal and professional, SMART), a daily and a weekly schedule (built with the day planner or their own tool), the priorities of one real week (the matrix), calendar blocks, two or three habits with a plan for each, how they will track progress without obsessing, and a short reflection. Allow AI help to design it, and require the learner to say what they changed.
- **P2 AI-Powered Productivity Assistant** (module 8, after day 35): a practical AI-assisted workflow the learner designs and runs, using **actual tools that exist**: Tali (the app's coach, a live model) and an assistant of their own choice (ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude, free versions are enough), with the AI Prompt Playground as a *checklist* (it is not an AI and does not run one: say so in the project text). The workflow: accept a list of tasks and requests; organise them by priority; make a daily plan; summarise a document or meeting notes; list action items; produce a weekly productivity report. The submission includes the prompts used, what the learner checked and corrected in each output, the final plan, and a reflection on what the AI got wrong. It must say plainly that **there is no in-app "assistant builder" simulation**: the learner builds the workflow from prompts and checks.
- **P3 The Productive Professional Challenge** (module 9, after day 39): a realistic scenario the learner must manage: several tasks, conflicting deadlines, meetings, client requests, personal responsibilities and an unexpected interruption. Give the full scenario in the project steps (write it out: names, times, what each client needs, what is fixed, what happens on Wednesday at 2 PM). The learner builds a practical schedule (they may use the planner), prioritises, **explains each decision** (what they did, delegated, postponed or declined and why), and writes the one message that renegotiates something. They may practise with the lessons' role-plays; steps that depend on a practice item name it by its title and say to complete it in the lesson where it appears.

## Language and rules
- **English only.** Write no Filipino or Tagalog (the owner's standing rule); plain, warm, simple English an ESL learner can follow. Short sentences. Define idioms.
- Never write dialogue or mascot lines for Tali or Tala.
- No invented statistics, studies or quotes. Nothing medical, legal or mental-health advice: where a situation calls for it (constant exhaustion, panic, a health problem), say plainly that a doctor or counsellor is the right person.
- Every example is believable and respectful.
- Do not give "the right plan". Show the principles and one or two good versions; the learner's own life decides.
- Report at the end: lessons written, activities by kind, planning activities (and their scenarios), practice items by kind, anything you were unsure about, the check output. Do not claim to have run the live AI engine; you cannot.
